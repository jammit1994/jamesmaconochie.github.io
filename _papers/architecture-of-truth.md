## Abstract

Two people read the same report and disagree. One concludes there is no fact of the matter, only competing perspectives. The other runs a careful analysis and issues a confident verdict on a question the evidence was never able to settle. These look like opposite failures. They are the same one: a claim that has come loose from whatever was holding it up.

This paper argues that a claim worth standing under is not something a person possesses but something an architecture produces. By architecture I mean everything standing behind a claim: the procedures that test it, the institutions that run those procedures, and the people those institutions have formed. Procedure is part of it. The part procedure cannot do is where this paper spends most of its time.

Because the parts can be named, the failures can be diagnosed rather than merely deplored. Four checks decide whether a claim is eligible: whether the thing it names as the cause really is the cause, whether people who had every reason to disagree agree anyway, whether anyone with the skill and the motive has tried to break it, and whether it survives time. The central claim of this paper is that these four checks are necessary but not enough. Passing them produces a candidate, not a conclusion. Somebody still has to recognise that a claim which has passed all four is strong enough for what it is about to be asked to carry, and no procedure performs that step.

Three institutional cases test the argument. Air-crash investigation shows the architecture working where little is contested. The Volcker disinflation shows it working under severe political pressure. The Federal Reserve in 2006 shows it substantially intact, staffed by capable people, with the information available, and the judgement not made. That third case is what the argument rests on. If sound architecture guaranteed sound judgement, there would be nothing here worth saying.

Two further arguments follow. Prediction markets, crowdsourcing and algorithmic ranking can feed the checks but cannot perform the judgement. And AI is changing the route by which almost everything now reaches a reader. The old route had people in it: reporters, editors, peer reviewers, named commentators. The new one runs through systems that assemble an answer out of text rather than by going and looking, and rules written for the old route will not catch what goes wrong in the new one.

This paper diagnoses the problem. It does not prescribe the cure. It proposes no institutions and no legislation; it sets out what any of them would have to respect.

## How This Fits

The Attention Crisis, The Wisdom Gap, and AHI were each built around something none of them stopped to defend. Attention was being defended for something. Wisdom was the mature form of something. AHI was an architecture toward something. That something was truth. Where the governance paper specified what such an architecture must do at the institutional level, this paper specifies what the architecture is for: what it produces when it works, and what it stops producing when it does not.
