![A conceptual triptych contrasting monolithic AI compute with the modular, efficient architecture of biological intelligence.](/assets/images/three_bridge_hero_1500by500.png)

My background is in civil engineering, a field that learned centuries ago that we build better structures through smarter architecture, not just more material. Suspension bridges span farther not because we found stronger stone, but because we discovered smarter ways to arrange materials, to let structure, not mass, do the work.

I see a similar inflection point in AI today.

The dominant approach assumes intelligence emerges from scale: more parameters, more data, more compute. But biological intelligence didn’t evolve that way, and I don’t believe artificial intelligence will, either.

The brain isn’t a monolithic processor that simply grew larger. It’s a distributed system of specialized modules. The true magic lies not in raw processing, but in coordination and feedback, how these components communicate, prioritize, and adapt.

That’s why I’m exploring what I call the architecture of attention: the design principles that let intelligence focus, reason, and adapt based on what matters in the moment.

---

**This is more than a technical shift; it's a philosophical one.**

The current race toward scaled-up LLMs often serves the interests of chip manufacturers more than genuine capability advancement. These systems are extraordinary, but they’re now colliding with architectural limits that more compute alone can’t overcome.

The alternative isn’t to abandon AI, but to rethink it from the ground up. This leads me to a critical distinction:

I’m focused on AHI (Augmented Human Intelligence) rather than AGI (Artificial General Intelligence).

This isn't just semantics, it's architectural. It’s about building systems designed to enhance human judgment rather than replace it.
